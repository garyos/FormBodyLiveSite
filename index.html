What drinks do to your body

Sugary soft drinks

Liquid calories don't fill you up the way food does, so they tend to add to what you eat rather than replace it. Adults are advised to keep free sugars under 30 g a day; one can of regular cola is about 35 g. Frequent sugary drinks also raise the risk of tooth decay.

Sugar-free and diet drinks

No sugar and almost no calories, so swapping from full-sugar versions is one of the simplest ways to cut intake. They're still acidic for teeth, and colas contain caffeine. Water remains the best everyday drink.

Juice and smoothies

Even with no added sugar, the sugar released from juicing or blending fruit counts as free sugar. 150 ml a day counts as one of your 5 a day, however much you drink. Whole fruit is more filling.

Milk, shakes and coffee

Milk brings protein and calcium, and its natural sugar isn't a free sugar. Lattes are mostly milk. Syrups, cream and blended iced drinks turn coffee into dessert: a large frappé can match a meal.

Caffeine and energy drinks

Up to about 400 mg a day is generally considered safe for most adults (200 mg if pregnant). Caffeine late in the day can disrupt sleep, which affects appetite and recovery. Energy drinks combine high caffeine with sugar unless sugar-free.

Alcohol

Alcohol has 7 kcal per gram, nearly as much as fat. UK guidance is no more than 14 units a week, spread over three or more days with several drink-free days. It disrupts sleep, lowers inhibitions around food, and heavy drinking after training can blunt muscle repair.

Figures are typical values for common UK serving sizes. Brands, measures and strengths vary, so check labels where you can. Units = strength (%) × volume (ml) ÷ 1,000.

How to progress

Own the reps

Use a full range you control. Early on, stop each set with 2–4 reps still in the tank (“reps in reserve”).

Double progression

Stay at a weight until every set hits the top of the rep range with clean form. Then add the smallest load jump, or a harder variation.

Recover

Rest 1–3 minutes on big lifts. If sleep, soreness and performance all slide, cut sets by about a third for a week.

What drives fat loss
Energy balance

A deficit drives weight loss

Across weeks, eating less energy than you use means stored energy makes up the gap. Many diets work if they create a deficit you can keep. As you get lighter, your expenditure falls a little too.

Lean tissue

Lifting plus protein

Resistance training signals your body to keep muscle; protein supplies the building blocks. A moderate deficit improves the odds that most of what you lose is fat.

Adherence

The best plan is repeatable

Food preferences, budget, culture, shifts and stress all matter. A perfect plan you can't follow does nothing.

Burning it off: what exercise really does with a meal

Every meal and drink on this site shows how far you'd have to run, walk, cycle, swim or lift to burn its energy. It's a way of making numbers feel real. Here's how those figures are worked out and what they do and don't mean.

The maths

Net calories, scaled to you

Figures use standard activity values (METs) from the Compendium of Physical Activities and count only the extra energy above what you'd burn sitting still. Running costs roughly 0.9 kcal per kg of body weight per km whatever your speed, so heavier people burn more per km.

Fat vs carbs

Why the split matters

Fat holds 9 kcal per gram, carbs and protein 4, alcohol 7. So 10 g of fat (two teaspoons of oil) is about 1.3 km of running for a 75 kg person, while 10 g of carbs is about 0.6 km. The split on each meal shows which part costs you most.

Lifting

Weights burn less than you'd think

A hard set plus rest takes about 3 minutes and burns roughly 15 kcal extra for a 75 kg person, so a 600 kcal takeaway is about 40 sets. Lifting earns its place by building and keeping muscle, not by burning the meal off.

You can't out-run a takeaway habit

A 900 kcal curry night is about a 13 km run for a 75 kg person. Exercise is brilliant for your heart, mood, sleep, blood sugar and keeping weight off, but cutting 500 kcal from a meal is usually far easier than finding the best part of an hour of running, or nearly three hours of walking, to burn it. People also tend to eat back some of what they burn and move less for the rest of the day without noticing.

Use it to compare, not to earn food

Most of what you eat each day fuels your body at rest: breathing, your brain, keeping warm. You don't need to burn off every meal. The useful number is the difference: a swap that saves 300 kcal is a 4 km run you never needed to do. If you notice yourself exercising to cancel out food, or feeling guilty about eating, talk to your GP or Beat (0808 801 0677).

Figures are estimates for steady effort. Fitness, terrain, pace, temperature and how hard you push all change the real numbers by 10–20% or more. Add your weight in Targets for figures scaled to you; otherwise they assume 75 kg.
Diets and fads: what actually works
Does an all-steak or keto diet really do the trick? Both can make you lose weight, but not through any special mechanism. They cut out whole food groups, high protein keeps you fuller, and the first few kilos are mostly water released as carbohydrate stores empty. In head-to-head trials, healthy low-carb and healthy low-fat diets give similar weight loss after a year. Keto has real medical uses and suits some appetites. The all-meat carnivore diet has almost no controlled research and real risks to cholesterol and gut health.
Mechanism

Every working diet creates a deficit

Diets get you there in different ways: banning food groups, limiting eating times, raising protein and fibre, or tracking. None beats energy balance; they're different routes to eating less.

Evidence

Head-to-head, results converge

In a large year-long US trial (DIETFITS), people on a healthy low-carb diet and a healthy low-fat diet lost similar amounts. Results varied hugely between people within each group, mostly down to how well they stuck to it.

Success

What predicts lasting results

A pattern you enjoy and can keep, enough protein and fibre, regular strength training, decent sleep, support or accountability, and a plan for maintenance after the diet ends.

How to spot a fad

  • Promises fast loss (more than about 1 kg a week) that lasts
  • Bans whole food groups without a medical reason
  • Says one food, gene, blood type or hormone is the secret
  • Talks about ‘toxins’, ‘detox’ or ‘resetting’ your body
  • Needs special products, teas or supplements
  • Relies on testimonials and before-and-after photos, not trials
  • Makes you feel guilty or ‘dirty’ about normal food

When to get help

Talk to your GP or a registered dietitian before a restrictive diet if you take medication for diabetes or blood pressure, have kidney, liver or heart disease, are pregnant or breastfeeding, are under 18, or have a history of eating disorders. If dieting is making you anxious about food, Beat (the UK eating disorder charity) can help: 0808 801 0677.

Balancing food groups for vitamins, minerals, protein and fats

No single food covers everything. Each food group brings a different set of nutrients, so the balance across a day and a week is what fills the gaps. This follows the UK's Eatwell Guide, adjusted for people training and changing body composition.

Fruit and vegetables · about 39%At least 5 portions (80 g each) a day, mostly veg, in a range of colours. Fibre, vitamin C, folate, potassium, polyphenols.
Starchy carbohydrates · about 37%Potatoes, bread, rice, pasta, oats. Choose wholegrain or higher-fibre. Energy for training, fibre, B vitamins, iron.

If you train hard or want to keep muscle in a deficit, the protein slice is often bigger than 12%: closer to a quarter of the plate at each meal, with the starchy slice trimmed on rest days.

Protein: how much and from where

The baseline for adults is about 0.75 g per kg of body weight a day. If you lift, are dieting to lose fat, or are over 65, 1.2–1.6 g per kg is a better target, and up to about 2 g per kg helps keep muscle in a hard deficit. Spread it over 3–4 meals of 20–40 g. Mix sources: animal proteins are complete; plant eaters get the full set by combining beans, lentils, grains, soya, nuts and seeds over the day.

Carbohydrates: quality and timing

Most carbs should come from wholegrains, potatoes with skins, beans, fruit and veg. Keep free sugars (added sugar, honey, syrups, juice) under 30 g a day. Put more of your starchy carbs around training and less on rest days if you're cutting.

Energy after eating: brain fog and the afternoon slump

Feeling sleepy, foggy or flat an hour or two after lunch is common. It usually comes from several things stacking up at once, and most of them are within your control.

Body clock

There's a natural dip

Alertness naturally dips in the early afternoon, roughly 1–3 pm, even if you skip lunch. Short sleep makes this dip much deeper, so food is only part of the picture.

Portion size

Big meals hit hardest

Large meals are the most consistent trigger of sleepiness after eating. Both very carb-heavy and very fatty meals are linked to it, and they're worst together: think pasty and crisps, or curry with naan and pilau.

Blood sugar

Fast rise, then a dip

Fast-digesting carbs with little protein or fibre cause a quick blood-sugar rise. In some people a dip follows 2–3 hours later, which research links to feeling hungry again and eating more. Protein, fibre and slower carbs flatten the curve.

What makes the slump worse

  • A lunch over about 800 kcal
  • White bread, baguettes, naan, pilau, chips, battered food, pastry and sticky or sushi rice as the main event
  • Sugary drinks, juice or a smoothie with the meal
  • Little protein (under 20 g) and little fibre
  • Alcohol at lunchtime
  • Eating fast at your desk, then sitting still
  • Short sleep, dehydration and too much caffeine late the day before

What helps

  • A lunch of roughly 400–600 kcal, with a protein snack later if you need it
  • 25–40 g protein and 8 g+ fibre: beans, lentils, wholegrains, vegetables
  • Half the plate vegetables or salad
  • Vegetables and protein first, carbs last
  • Water or sugar-free drinks
  • A 10–15 minute walk after eating, ideally outside in daylight
  • Caffeine before about 2 pm only
  • Consistent sleep

Is it calories, or the type of fat?

Short answer: calories decide whether you gain or lose weight. The type of fat (and the type of carbs and protein) decides how full you feel, how easy it is to overeat, and what happens to your heart and metabolic health. You need both lenses.

Weight

Energy balance drives it

Over weeks, eating more energy than you burn leads to weight gain, whether the extra comes from fat, carbs, protein or alcohol. Calorie for calorie, fat isn't more fattening than carbs.

Density

But fat packs calories in

Fat has 9 kcal a gram, alcohol 7, carbs and protein 4. That's why a free-poured glug of oil (about 360 kcal), butter on toast or a creamy sauce can quietly double a meal without making it look bigger.

Fullness

Not every calorie fills you equally

Protein is the most filling nutrient and takes the most energy to digest. Fibre adds bulk. Liquid calories and very processed, energy-dense snacks are the easiest to overeat: in one controlled US trial, people ate about 500 kcal a day more on an ultra-processed diet.

Saturated vs unsaturated fat

Same calories, different effects. Saturated fat, found in butter, ghee, cheese, cream, fatty and processed meat, pastry, palm and coconut oil, raises LDL cholesterol, a major cause of heart disease. Unsaturated fat in olive and rapeseed oil, nuts, seeds, avocado and oily fish helps lower LDL when it replaces saturated fat. UK guidance: no more than 30 g of saturated fat a day for men and 20 g for women.

Does fat type change where fat is stored?

Possibly. In one small overfeeding trial, extra calories from saturated fat (palm oil) increased liver and visceral (deep belly) fat more than the same extra calories from polyunsaturated fat (sunflower oil), with similar weight gain. It's early evidence, but it points the same way as the heart evidence: choose unsaturated fats.

Sugar and salt

Sugar has the same 4 kcal a gram as other carbs, but free sugars in drinks and sweets are easy to overconsume and don't fill you. Salt has no calories, but it holds water, so a salty takeaway can show up as a heavier scale reading the next day, and it raises blood pressure. The limit is 6 g a day.

What to do with this

Use calories to steer weight. Use protein and fibre to stay full. Swap saturated fats for unsaturated ones where you can, and measure cooking fat rather than pouring it. The ingredient guide shows all three for each food.

Trans fats are the most harmful type, but most UK manufacturers removed artificial trans fats years ago; they're now rare in UK foods.
Three kinds of body fat

Subcutaneous

Under the skin. It shapes how you look and stores energy. Not all of it is harmful; distribution and overall health matter.

Visceral

Deep in the abdomen around the organs. Higher levels are linked to insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes and heart disease. Waist size is a useful but rough proxy.

Ectopic (organ)

Fat in the liver, muscle or pancreas. Fatty liver can be clinically important; a clinician assesses it with bloods and imaging.

You can't choose where fat comes off. Ab exercises strengthen your core but don't burn belly fat. Overall fat loss, regular activity, lifting, diet quality, sleep and less alcohol improve metabolic health. Visceral fat often responds fairly quickly to sustained change.

Heart and circulation

Swap some saturated fat (fatty or processed meat, butter, pastries) for unsaturated fats (olive or rapeseed oil, nuts, seeds, oily fish). Choose oats, beans, lentils, fruit and vegetables. No single food clears arteries.

Can fat turn into muscle?

No. They are different tissues. Recomposition means losing fat and gaining muscle at the same time, which works best for beginners, people returning after a break, and people with more fat to lose.

Meal timing that fits your life

Anchor meals to waking

A consistent pattern helps. Shift workers: eat your main meal in your biological daytime where you can, and plan lighter food overnight instead of grazing.

Spread protein out

Aim for 3–4 protein-containing meals, often 25–40 g each. Daily total matters more than a narrow post-workout window.

Protect sleep

Finish big meals 2–3 hours before bed if reflux or poor sleep is an issue. Keep caffeine to the first half of your waking day.

Eating earlier in the day may modestly help some metabolic markers, but trials are mixed and the effect is smaller than total intake, food quality and consistency. Fasting is an optional structure, not a metabolic upgrade.

0.25–1% of bodyweight a week is a sustainable range. Leaner or highly trained people should favour the slower end.

A small surplus of about 100–250 kcal a day. Watch the monthly trend and your waist. Faster scale gain is not more muscle.

Eat near maintenance, lift consistently and hit protein. Judge by waist, photos and performance together.

Before using calorie targets

Estimates can be off by several hundred calories, so don't treat them as a hard ceiling. Avoid very-low-calorie diets unless clinically supervised. Get tailored advice if you're under 18, pregnant or breastfeeding, frail, on glucose-lowering medication, have kidney or liver disease, or have a current or past eating disorder.

When to see a clinician

Unexplained weight change, suspected sleep apnoea, high blood pressure, diabetes risk, persistent fatigue or pain. Chest pain, fainting or severe breathlessness during exercise need urgent assessment.
